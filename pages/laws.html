Every trade keeps commandments. Ours are mostly scar tissue with punchlines — the collected paranoia of people who have shipped something to production and lived to be sarcastic about it. Where the record names an author, credit is given.1 The rest belong to folklore, which in engineering carries the force of law anyway.

Debugging

  • If it can fail, it will fail.2
  • No matter how well it works, assume it’s broken somewhere.
  • It’s always your fault until it’s provably not your fault.
  • If you didn’t test it, it doesn’t work.
  • If you only tested it once, it doesn’t work.
  • If it works on your machine, that’s where it belongs.3
  • The bug is never where you first think it is.
  • The harder you insist “that can’t be the problem,” the more likely it’s the problem.
  • Trust, but verify. Then verify the verification.4

Operations

  • Everything fails eventually. Design accordingly.5
  • Every system is a distributed system once enough time passes.
  • Every distributed system is eventually a messaging system.
  • Every success story is built on a pile of postmortems.
  • Production is the world’s most expensive test environment.
  • There are two kinds of systems: those that are down, and those that haven’t gone down yet.
  • Nothing is temporary. Especially temporary fixes.

Ownership

  • The problem belongs to whoever discovered it until it belongs to someone else.
  • Don’t prove it’s someone else’s problem. Prove it’s not yours.
  • Users don’t care whose team owns the bug.
  • The customer experiences one system, not seventeen microservices.

Design

  • Make it work. Make it right. Make it fast. In that order.6
  • Every abstraction leaks eventually.7
  • All models are wrong; some are useful.8
  • Simple things should be simple. Complex things should be possible.9
  • The cleverer the code, the less likely Future You will understand it.10

Meetings and Process

  • Adding people to a late project makes it later.11
  • The best documentation is the code. The second-best documentation is documentation.
  • Every process exists because something terrible happened once.
  • The most dangerous words in engineering are: “Nobody would ever do that.”

Senior Engineer

  • Assume the logs are lying. Then check them anyway.
  • Read the error message. Actually read it.
  • Before building a solution, make sure you understand the problem.
  • The issue you’ve spent six hours chasing is probably a typo.
  • The issue that looks like a typo probably isn’t.

Commandments

  • It is always your fault until proven otherwise.
  • If it isn’t monitored, it isn’t running.
  • If it isn’t tested, it doesn’t work.
  • If it isn’t documented, it doesn’t exist.
  • If it can’t fail, you haven’t found the failure mode yet.
  • If it works perfectly, you’re missing something.
  • The customer will always find the one workflow you never tested.12
  • Future You is a different person and deserves kindness.13
  • The root cause is usually human, even when the symptom is technical.
  • Production gets a vote.

  1. Researched October 2026. “Attributed” below means the saying is universally associated with the name but no primary source survives — the usual condition of engineering law.↩︎

  2. Murphy’s Law. Capt. Edward A. Murphy Jr., 1949, after a miswired strain gauge during the MX981 rocket-sled tests at what is now Edwards Air Force Base.↩︎

  3. Folklore. The joke predates Docker; Docker (2013) turned “then we’ll ship your machine” into a business model.↩︎

  4. Russian proverb — doveryai, no proveryai — quoted by Ronald Reagan to Mikhail Gorbachev at the Höfði House meeting, Reykjavík, 1986.↩︎

  5. Werner Vogels, AWS CTO (2008): “Everything fails, all the time.”↩︎

  6. Attributed to Kent Beck (extreme programming), via the original c2 wiki.↩︎

  7. Joel Spolsky, “The Law of Leaky Abstractions”, Joel on Software, 11 November 2002: “All non-trivial abstractions, to some degree, are leaky.”↩︎

  8. George E. P. Box. The famous short form appears in Box & Draper, Empirical Model-Building and Response Surfaces (1987); the 1976 paper everyone cites (“Science and Statistics,” JASA) contains the long form.↩︎

  9. Attributed to Alan Kay.↩︎

  10. Cf. Brian Kernighan, The Elements of Programming Style (2nd ed., with P. J. Plauger), ch. 2: “Everyone knows that debugging is twice as hard as writing a program in the first place. So if you’re as clever as you can be when you write it, how will you ever debug it?”↩︎

  11. Brooks’s Law. Fred Brooks, The Mythical Man-Month (1975): “Adding manpower to a late software project makes it later.”↩︎

  12. Cf. Hyrum’s Law, Hyrum Wright: “With a sufficient number of users of an API, it does not matter what you promise in the contract: all observable behaviors of your system will be depended on by somebody.”↩︎

  13. In that spirit: “Always code as if the guy who ends up maintaining your code will be a violent psychopath who knows where you live.” — John Woods, comp.lang.c++, September 1991. Routinely misattributed to Martin Golding.↩︎
